Thankful Villages: luck or size?

About fifty-five parishes in England and Wales sent men to the First World War and got every one of them back. Were they lucky, or just small?

Tristan Hotham · Data-led researcher of politics, the past, platforms and places · 2026

One in eight

Arthur Mee coined "Thankful Village" in the 1930s for a place that lost no one in the Great War. A 2013 update by researchers identified 52 civil parishes. The list kept on Wikipedia, which I use here, now has 55, three of them in Wales. No Thankful Village has yet been found in Scotland or Ireland. The list also marks 18 villages as "doubly thankful", having lost no one in the Second World War either, though not all of those are verified.

Roughly one in eight men who served from the United Kingdom died. If each man's fate were an independent coin toss weighted at 12%, the chance of everyone coming home falls fast with every extra man. Five of the villages record how many served. For each of them, getting everyone back was something between a 1-in-4 and a 1-in-25 shot.

The chance that every man comes home if each faces an independent 12% risk of death. Dots mark the five Thankful Villages whose memorials or histories give the number who served.

Small helps a lot

Size is most of the story. I matched each Thankful Village to its modern civil parish and its 2011 Census population. Parishes under 500 people today are about nine times likelier to be on the list than parishes of 1,000 or more. The few larger ones, such as East Wittering, Flixborough and Stoke Hammond, have mostly grown since 1918.

But chance predicts far more

Here is the puzzle. Run the coin-toss model over every small parish in England and Wales and it predicts hundreds of Thankful Villages, not 55. With between 6 and 13 men serving for every 100 people today, the expected total runs from about to . Most of them would be in hamlets of a few hundred people or fewer.

Some of the gap is my proxy. Modern parish populations are not 1911 ones. Still, no reasonable correction gets the model down to 55. Two other explanations fit better.

First, a civil parish is not a community. Many tiny civil parishes were townships of a larger church parish, and their men's names went on that parish's memorial. A hamlet that lost no one is invisible on a memorial shared with places that did.

Second, thankfulness has to be proved. A parish joins the list only when researchers can show who served and that all came back. A memorial with names on it is evidence. The absence of one is not.

So the answer to "luck or size?" is both, and something more. Being small made it possible. Luck made it happen. And record-keeping decided which villages we remember as thankful. There are very likely many more.

    Method

    1. The list of 55 villages (52 in England, 3 in Wales) and their "doubly thankful" status come from Wikipedia's Thankful Villages article, which follows the research of Norman Edwards and Tom Morgan. Coordinates come from each village's Wikipedia article.
    2. Each village was placed in its modern civil parish by its nearest postcodes (postcodes.io). Where the modern parish has a different name, the village has been merged into a larger parish, and I left it out of the size comparison.
    3. Populations are 2011 Census usual residents by parish (KS101EW, via Nomis). This is the latest Census published for parishes, but it is only a proxy for 1911.
    4. The luck model gives each serving man a 12% chance of death. A parish of N people sends s·N men, so it is thankful with probability 0.88s·N. I tried s from 0.06 to 0.13, which brackets the served-to-resident ratios of the five villages with known counts.
    5. The five served counts come from the village articles: Upper Slaughter (24 men and one woman), Colwinston (23), Nether Kellet (21), Harley (20) and Stretton en le Field (11).
